# Brief: a line to borrow for each Wordhoard word (2026-10-12)

Wordhoard is a private daily vocabulary practice for Leandre Jack (Georgetown, Guyana; supervises a finance unit at a US embassy; MSc in Oil and Gas; he learns by ear: every word on his Wordhoard site has a recorded pronunciation button, and he listens on his phone). The data is /Users/leandrejack/projects/Wordhoard/lexicon.json: 30 days x 3 words. Each word has id, word, pos, definition, nuance, three examples (examples.conversational / professional / elevated), collocations, registerNote; each day has a passage and an epigram.

His request: "the same way I can click to hear the word pronounced, I would like to have that option of clicking and hearing it being used in an example... but it must be the type of beauty in writing that I like, where I can borrow the phraseology, the terminology and so forth... so I can learn it faster, learn it better and understand context."

For each word in your share, write one "line to borrow": a single beautifully made line that uses the word. He will hear it in a recorded voice (a button beside the pronunciation button, and a link in his daily email) and lift phrases from it into his own speech and writing.

## What he likes
- Learned words used precisely, with a punchline or a turn at the end: "the mark of a thoughtful writer... that punch of wow".
- He has rejected flat, choppy "plain language" three times; he wants cadence and craft.
- He hears machine copy at once and dislikes it: leverage, streamline, empower, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, delve, tapestry, testament to, navigate, landscape, realm, journey, unlock, elevate, transform, "brings", "not just X, but Y", "in today's world", and lines that end on a tidy moral.
- His own formal writing: "I had to make three choices when writing this into code. Here is my rationale." / "a set has no bearing on the true question" / "I bear responsibility for the content herein, and I'm willing to modify my approach if that is preferred."

## These are the standard to match (thirty lines already accepted for other words)
- "An exhaustive list of my mistakes would take the evening, so I will offer the three that taught me something and spare you the ones that merely cost money."
- "The name stayed elusive all evening and arrived, of course, in the car on the way home, with nobody left to tell it to."
- "I treat gossip the way a dealer treats a painting: no provenance, no price, however pretty the frame."
- "Some problems are intractable only because the two people who could solve them have never been seated at the same table, and that is a problem of chairs."
- "She was tenacious in the quiet way, never raising her voice, simply returning to the same office every week with the same folder until the folder won."
- "The figures were unassailable. I learned that afternoon that a room can agree with every number on the page and still vote no."

## Rules for each line
- "text": 14 to 38 words, one or two sentences, ending with a full stop (or a question mark).
  * Uses the headword exactly once, in the exact sense and part of speech the lexicon teaches. Respect the definitions: plethora is an excess, not merely many; obviate means to remove the need for; burgeon is to begin to grow and flourish; hone means to sharpen, never "hone in"; demur is to voice hesitation or objection; umbrage lives in "take umbrage at"; intransigent, truculent, recalcitrant and obstinate are negative, so aim them at a situation or a third party, never at the listener; consummate and articulate are the adjectives here.
  * The bare headword, or only a regular ending: verbs + s / ed / d (no -ing on verbs ending in e, like "galvanizing"); adjectives + ly only where the adverb is common; nouns + s. Never a derived form (no "eloquence" for "eloquent").
  * Reads like a line from a fine essay, a well-made speech or a novel's narration: cadence and balance, a concrete image, wit, a turn or a sting at the end.
  * Sayable aloud by a professional man in conversation, a toast, a meeting, a eulogy, a negotiation or a well-written email, without pomposity. International register; no US-only references; no politics, religion, embassy or diplomacy.
  * Plain words around the headword: no second rare word unless it is another of the 90 Wordhoard words used correctly. No cliches.
  * New: do not repeat or paraphrase that word's three existing examples, its day's passage or epigram.
  * Original: no quotations, no allusions to named people, no famous lines reworked, no personal names.
  * No em or en dashes (use commas, colons, semicolons, full stops), no digits, no abbreviations, no exclamation marks, no quotation marks inside the line.
- "borrow": the reusable phrase inside the line, 3 to 8 words, an EXACT substring of "text" (same case and punctuation) that contains the headword: the most quotable, most reusable chunk.
- "setting": 3 to 9 words, sentence case, no full stop: where he might say or write it. Vary these widely.
- Variety: vary the shapes (aphorism, a scene in one sentence, a compliment, a polite rebuke, an observation about people, a toast, a sentence from an email, a line of narration). Avoid repeating templates like "X is not Y; it is Z".
